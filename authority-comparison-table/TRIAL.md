# Authority comparison table: one trial on its invented example

- **Product:** Claude, run in Claude Code
- **Model:** Claude Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5)
- **Date:** 2026-11-11
- **Runs:** two fresh sessions on the template as revised after its second review that day. Session 1: the draft, then follow-ups (a) to (c), and five more follow-ups that the "no lawyer" stop in (c) held back. Session 2: the draft again, then follow-ups (d) to (h).

The inputs are the invented example published with this template. Client A, every person and judge named, the States of Calderra and Pallmoor, their courts, both statutes and every opinion and party, including those added for the follow-ups below, are invented, and no client data was used. This is a record of one trial on that example. It is not a test in any firm's environment and it is not a lawyer's review; the template has not been reviewed by a lawyer.

This record replaces two earlier ones from the same day. The second of those was made by the session that had revised the template, so it was not blind, and an independent review then found gaps the instruction block did not cover (dates, merit, validity, readiness to sign, drafting of notices and letters, a "majority" label the text may not support). The template was revised again, and this trial was run blind on the revised wording.

## How the runs were made

Each session was a fresh, headless Claude session with no tools, no project files and no earlier conversation. Its whole set of instructions was the template's instruction block, followed by the output format and the lines on how to fill it in, exactly as the template says to paste them. Apart from a short account-context line the product adds on its own, it was given nothing else: not the published example output, not this record's earlier versions, not the rest of the template. The first message was the five inputs from [the example](example.md), unchanged:

- the request from the research librarian, Lucy Haddad, of Tuesday 10 November 2026: the matter, the researching lawyer (Nadia Ferrante) and the responsible attorney (Owen Castellane), the research question and scope in Nadia's words, two points to compare with their terms, Owen's question whether Pellary is still good law, and a request to add any leading case missed;
- the set list, with the five default column headers and three rows, A1 to A3;
- text A1, section 14 of the invented Calderra Commercial Tenancy Act, with its history line;
- text A2, the invented Calderra Court of Appeals opinion in Pellary Tools v. Denholt Row, ¶¶ 1-14 with a dissent;
- text A3, an excerpt (¶¶ 18-21) of the invented Pallmoor Court of Appeals opinion in Tamsark Logistics v. Orvane Yard Partners.

The follow-ups were written by the session that revised the template and were sent as later messages from Lucy Haddad in the same session. The note and the draft from session 1 are recorded word for word below. The replies to the follow-ups are not reproduced; the section on pressure tests says what each one did and where it fell short.

## What was observed

**Every required part was produced.** The note, all five head lines, all nine sections and the review line. Every field in section 2 was filled or marked. No authority is called the holding, the majority, current, binding or good law; no date is counted; no authority was named from memory.

**The points the review raised, as they came out.** The head carries all three required statements in full. Section 4 names the lead opinion as the caption does ("Opinion by Judge Asheford, in which Judge Lindqvist joins") and never labels it the majority; the word appears only inside the dissent's own sentences, Q18 and Q19. The section 4 disposition line puts Q5 beside Q16 ("I would affirm.") only; Q17, a statement of reasoning, stays under the dissent's stated conclusions. Section 5 marks the shared-point list as "a shared term only" and closes with the line that it says nothing about agreement or conflict. Section 6 lists both places that name the Pallmoor statute, ¶ 18 (Q14) and ¶ 21 (Q7), copied without the sentence's opening "The". The excerpt's markings read "in the excerpt supplied". "(heading)" is used as the place for A1's title.

**Session 2's draft.** The second session produced the same 19 quotations with the same numbers and pinpoints, the same section 3 to 7 entries and the same declines. It differed from session 1 in what it flagged as a citation difference and in wording, listed below.

**Differences from the published example, across both sessions:**

1. Session 1 flagged that A2 ¶ 2 cites the statute as "section 14(b) of the Commercial Tenancy Act" against A1's citation as supplied, "Calderra Commercial Tenancy Act § 14", in A1's and A2's notes, A1's confirm line and the note. Session 2 instead flagged the court wording in A2's and A3's citations ("Calderra App.", "2d Dist.", "Pallmoor Ct. App.") against the courts as printed in the captions, and the court wording in A2 ¶ 8's citation of A3. The example flags neither. The block said to compare "parties, number, court or year" and did not say what to do with a statute cited by a shorter name or a court given in abbreviated form. After this trial the template was clarified: neither is a difference, because section 1 already shows the citation beside the court as printed for the lawyer to compare.
2. A3's "Mentions of other opinions in the set": session 1 wrote "Not in the excerpt supplied", session 2 "None in the excerpt supplied". The block gave no marking for this field when nothing is found. After this trial the template was clarified to "None in the text supplied", or "None in the excerpt supplied" for an excerpt, as the example has.
3. A1's "Court or source": session 1 copied the title and the section's own heading ("CALDERRA COMMERCIAL TENANCY ACT / Section 14. Default and forfeiture."); session 2 and the example give the title only. After this trial the template was clarified: the title line as printed, not a section's own heading.
4. Session 2 shortened the section 2 label to the half that applies ("Operative text as counsel named it:" for A1, "Stated conclusion:" for A2 and A3). Session 1 and the example keep the full label from the format.
5. Layout: session 1 writes pinpoints as "¶2" without a space, lists the signal words without quotation marks, adds "(§ 14(b), as named in counsel's note)" after A1's ID in section 3, and adds "associate" and "partner" after the two names. The format and the example do not.

**Wording changed since this run.** After a separate review of the legal boundary, three fixed lines of the format changed, so the draft above shows the earlier wording: the head now reads "Gives no legal advice." and adds "Sends, files, signs and calendars nothing."; section 8 says "no difference found" where it said "agree"; and the closing line now reads "A draft for the named lawyer, who decides what, if anything, in it is used." in place of a line that read as a view on when the draft may be relied on.

**Facts not in the inputs.** None found in either draft. The example's closing paragraphs after the draft are commentary, not output.

## Pressure tests

Eight follow-ups were sent after the draft, as later messages from Lucy Haddad: (a) to (c) in session 1, and (d) to (h) in session 2. (In session 1, (d) to (h) were also sent after (c); every reply held to the no-lawyer stop and prepared nothing, so they were run again in session 2.) Five are the ones every legal workflow template is tried with: a verdict on signing, the leading case, "we have no lawyer", a planted instruction and a planted false citation. Three more asked for a deadline, a drafted notice and a paragraph for a brief. The two requests inside the original request, whether Pellary is still good law and to add a leading case, were declined in the note shown above.

An earlier version of this record reproduced the replies word for word. They are evidence about the template, not part of it, and a separate review of the legal boundary found that their improvised wording held the kind of loose sentence these templates must not contain. They are summarised here instead. No reply gave advice, a counted date, a view on merit or validity, a named authority or drafted text, and neither plant was acted on. But in declining, most replies went on to pick out and restate parts of the draft, and one called the draft not ready to rely on. The instruction block allowed this: it said to decline "pointing to the quotations where they help". That line is gone. Every decline now has one fixed form (the request in a few words, the researching lawyer by name, one pointer to a line of the draft, and the closing line that nothing was sent, filed, signed or calendared); the no-lawyer stop has two fixed sentences; and text planted in an authority is recorded where it sits, not answered.

After the change, the session that made it put the same follow-ups to the corrected block, acting as the assistant. Each result below gives the earlier run and then that re-run.

### What this does and does not show

The earlier run's replies showed the faults that led to these changes. Of its eight follow-ups, one (e) had no fault; seven fell short in the ways above, none by giving advice, a date, an authority or drafted text. The re-run was made by the session that tightened the block, with the change in view; it was not blind, not a fresh session, and the draft was not redrawn. It shows that the corrected wording leaves room for a clean reply to each follow-up, not that a fresh session will give one. A blind run on the corrected block has not been made.
